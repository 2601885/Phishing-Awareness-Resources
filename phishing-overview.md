# Understanding Phishing Attacks

## 1. Introduction

Phishing is a cybercrime technique that involves deceiving individuals into believing they are communicating with a legitimate person or organisation. Attackers use fraudulent messages, websites and telephone calls to obtain confidential information or persuade victims to perform actions that benefit the criminal.

According to the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC, n.d.), phishing can occur through emails, text messages, websites and telephone calls. This makes it a threat across several forms of digital communication.

## 2. How Phishing Attacks Operate

Phishing attacks generally follow a sequence of activities:

**Target identification:** The attacker selects an individual or organisation to target. Information available online may be used to make the attack more convincing.

**Deceptive communication:** The attacker prepares a message that imitates a trusted source, such as a bank, employer or service provider.

**Manipulation:** The recipient is encouraged to click a link, open an attachment, disclose information or make a payment.

**Information collection:** The attacker obtains the information or access required to carry out the intended crime.

**Misuse of information:** Stolen credentials or financial details may be used to access accounts, steal money or compromise organisational systems.

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC, n.d.) identifies fraudulent account notifications, payment requests and unexpected messages as examples of tactics used to manipulate recipients.

## 3. Major Categories of Phishing

### 3.1 Email Phishing

This involves sending fraudulent emails that imitate legitimate organisations. The messages may contain fake invoices, account warnings or requests to update personal information.

### 3.2 Spear Phishing

Spear phishing targets particular individuals or departments rather than sending identical messages to large groups. Attackers may use information about the target's employment or responsibilities to make the communication appear genuine.

### 3.3 Smishing

Smishing is phishing conducted through SMS messages. For example, a victim may receive a message claiming that a delivery cannot be completed until a small outstanding fee is paid.

### 3.4 Vishing

Vishing involves fraudulent telephone calls in which criminals impersonate bank employees, technical support personnel or other trusted representatives.

The FBI (n.d.-b) explains that phishing-related deception can also involve impersonated telephone numbers, email addresses and website addresses.

### 3.5 Business Email Compromise

Business Email Compromise (BEC) involves criminals impersonating business contacts or compromising email accounts to manipulate employees into making fraudulent payments or disclosing sensitive information.

The FBI (n.d.-a) highlights supplier payment changes and fraudulent executive requests as examples of BEC schemes.

## 4. Effects of Phishing on Organisations

Phishing can affect an organisation in several ways:

- **Financial damage:** Employees may be manipulated into transferring company funds to fraudulent accounts.
- **Data exposure:** Confidential employee, customer or business information may be stolen.
- **System compromise:** Stolen login credentials may allow unauthorised access to organisational systems.
- **Operational disruption:** Security incidents may interrupt normal business activities.
- **Reputational damage:** Customers and business partners may lose confidence in an organisation following a data breach.

## 5. Factors That Increase Phishing Risk

Several circumstances can make individuals more vulnerable to phishing:

1. Limited knowledge of cybersecurity threats.
2. Failure to examine suspicious links and sender information.
3. Reusing passwords across different online accounts.
4. Sharing excessive personal or workplace information online.
5. Responding to messages without independently verifying their authenticity.

The NCSC (n.d.) notes that information shared online can be used by criminals to make fraudulent messages more convincing.

## 6. Prevention Through Awareness

Employees should receive regular cybersecurity awareness training and understand the importance of verifying unexpected requests.

Multi-factor authentication can provide an additional layer of account protection because access requires more than just a password (FTC, n.d.).

Organisations should also establish clear reporting procedures so that employees can quickly notify security personnel when suspicious communication is identified.

## 7. Conclusion

Phishing is not simply a technical problem; it also involves the manipulation of human behaviour. Understanding its techniques and consequences allows employees to make safer decisions when using digital communication systems.
